# Digest C-r1-1: Planning horizon vs cumulative lead time, end-of-horizon effects, low-level coding

Researcher run: 2026-11-02. Budget used: 14 tool calls. Six sources were actually read (fetched or text-extracted): Gershwin MIT slides, Gardner chapter, van den Heuvel & Wagelmans 2002, the Fisher et al. 2001 abstract, the Baker & Peterson 1979 abstract, and Stadtler 2000 as described in a thesis. Several more appear only as search-result snippets. **No priority textbook was retrieved this run**: no Jacobs et al. 6th ed., no Ptak & Smith, no Orlicky 1975, and no ASCM Dictionary entry. Each claim below says whether it was read in full or seen only as a snippet.

## Findings

1. **Cumulative lead time = the longest path through the BOM.** It is also called "aggregate lead time, stacked lead time, composite lead time, critical path lead time". It is found by "reviewing each bill of material path below the item, and whichever path adds up to the greatest number defines cumulative material lead time." | Glossary: Cumulative Lead Time | Oliver Wight Americas | n.d. | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://www.oliverwight-americas.com/glossary-terms/cumulative-lead-time/ | confidence: medium. The wording matches APICS Dictionary style, but the page fetch returned no definition, so this comes from the search snippet only. | definition | secondhand (consultancy glossary, likely paraphrasing APICS)

2. **Rule: the horizon must cover the longest cumulative lead time.** The planning horizon is "the length of time into the future (number of periods or days) for which the planning system will generate requirements. The horizon should be set long enough to accommodate the longest cumulative lead time for any item in the population." | Glossary: planning horizon | Cargoz | n.d. | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://www.cargoz.com/glossary/planning-horizon-1506 | confidence: medium for the rule (the same wording appears in several glossaries and agrees with the APICS-style phrasing), low for this source (logistics-vendor glossary, search snippet only) | textbook-rule / definition | secondhand

3. **What happens when the horizon is too short.** Another secondary source says the horizon "must, at minimum, equal the cumulative lead time of the longest product; otherwise, the system cannot release orders early enough to meet future demand." A flashcard set adds: "Shorter planning horizon may result in less-effective plans if significant future demand is not visible." | slm.mba glossary page; Brainscape "MRP Flashcards" | n.d. | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://slm.mba/?p=8214 ; https://brainscape.com/flashcards/mrp-7645427/packs/12354668 | confidence: low (unsourced web pages, snippets only) | textbook-rule | secondhand

4. **Past-due release = infeasible plan, flagged as an exception.** A textbook-style MRP spreadsheet (8-week planning horizon in its example) prints "Not enough leadtime to meet net rq. in week 2" when the lead-time offset would push a release before week 1. The text says the firm "must adjust the master schedule that generated the gross requirements or find some other way to avoid the leadtime problem." The model also "automatically checks the first 6 weeks of the schedule for leadtime problems" and errors if lead time is "greater than 6 weeks". It is single-level: there is no cumulative check across levels. | Ch. 4 "Material Requirements Planning" (MRP1 spreadsheet), OM course text | Everette S. Gardner Jr., Univ. of Houston Bauer College | n.d. | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://www.bauer.uh.edu/egardner/3301H%20Operations%20Management/OM%20Text/4MRP-1.pdf | confidence: medium | textbook-rule (teaching model) | primary (author's own course text; not peer-reviewed)

5. **Some ERPs simply do not plan past-due components or their children.** A practitioner forum on BPCS ERP: "If a new order is entered in violation of lead times such that one or more components would be past due at time of first MRP, then MRP will not plan those components or their BOM children." | BPCS-L mailing list archive | midrange.com | ca. 1999–2002 | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://archive.midrange.com/bpcs-l/199910/msg00113.html | confidence: low (one practitioner, one system, snippet only) | empirical (system behaviour) | secondhand

6. **Low-level code, as defined in lecture notes.** Items get "a low-level code corresponding to the lowest level they appear at, for any end item in the factory." 7. **Common parts are netted once, after all gross requirements are combined.** "MRP processes items in order of low-level code so that all gross requirements for a part are aggregated before the system calculates its net requirements. Without this rule, the system would order the part multiple times for the same period." | Search snippet. 8. **A parent's planned order releases become the child's gross requirements.** MIT lecture notes (Gershwin, citing Hopp & Spearman), as returned in search: "After scheduling production for all the top level (Level 0) items, do the same for Level 1 items. 9. **End-of-horizon effects in lot sizing.** "Because of end-effects, the optimal solution of the short model horizon lot sizing problem may not be the optimal solution in the long run. In particular, in a lot sizing problem it is always optimal to leave zero inventory at the end of the model horizon." One remedy is to "extend the short model horizon by using expected demand for the additional periods" (Extended Wagner-Whitin), and "it will often suffice to extend the short model horizon with only a limited number of periods", citing Federgruen & Tzur (1994). | "A Note on Ending Inventory Valuation in Multiperiod Production Scheduling", ERIM Report ERS-2002-63-LIS | W. van den Heuvel & A.P.M. 10. **Ending-inventory valuation (Fisher et al. 2001).** Short-horizon models set end-of-horizon inventory to zero, which causes extra setups or stockouts later. The fix is to add a valuation term for ending inventory to the objective. In rolling-horizon tests it beat Wagner-Whitin and Silver-Meal "for modest to long horizons". | "Ending Inventory Valuation in Multiperiod Production Scheduling", Management Science 47(5):679–692 | M. ## Disagreements between sources

- **What "minimum horizon" means.** The glossaries (findings 2–3) say horizon ≥ longest cumulative lead time. A practitioner SAP answer (search snippet, not read) says horizon = cumulative lead time + longest batching period, which is stricter once lot sizing groups several periods. Under lot-for-lot the two coincide.
- **What to do with infeasible (past-due) requirements.** Gardner (finding 4) flags them and puts the fix on the MPS. BPCS (finding 5) silently does not plan the component or its children. Neither source describes the "move to period 1 and explode" convention the brief asks about, so this run found no evidence for or against it.
- **End effects: horizon length vs remedy.** Baker & Peterson and the Extended Wagner-Whitin result favour longer or extended horizons. Fisher et al. favour valuing terminal inventory. Van den Heuvel & Wagelmans argue Fisher et al.'s advantage comes "to a large extent" from assuming knowledge of future demand. All of this work is single-level lot sizing, not multi-level MRP explosion.

## Implications for the hypothesis (interpretation, not evidence)

- Every rule retrieved is stated in terms of **cumulative** (critical-path) lead time, not single-item L. A check that fires only when a single item's L ≥ H does not implement the rule in findings 1–3. With 3 levels and H = 8–12 weeks, a path with, say, L = 4+4+4 breaks the rule without triggering the single-item message.
- Requirements for lowest-level parts that come from end-item demand inside the last (cumulative LT) periods of the horizon will offset to before period 1 (past due), or their own releases fall outside the visible window. The retrieved sources treat the former as an exception to report (Gardner). They also treat a too-short horizon as making demand "not visible" (finding 3).
