Researcher run 2026-11-02. Sources: Crossref API (bibliographic records), OpenAlex API (abstracts and citation chasing), Emerald and IAOR landing pages, one web search. Semantic Scholar returned HTTP 429 (rate limit) on most calls. Every finding below comes from a record or abstract retrieved in this run. No full texts were read. 4. **Blackburn, Kropp & Millen (1986): change cost or freezing dominate.**
   - Nervousness is especially disruptive in multi-level processes, because a change at one level changes other levels.
   - The study tests five strategies: freezing within the horizon, lot-for-lot after stage 1, safety stock, forecasting beyond the horizon, and change cost.
   - When nervousness comes from the rolling horizon, safety stock and lot-for-lot are not cost-effective.
   - Change cost and freezing "appear to be dominant" under most conditions.
   - Demand, supply and lead-time uncertainty were explicitly excluded.

   | Blackburn, J.D., Kropp, D.H., Millen, R.A. (1986). A comparison of strategies to dampen nervousness in MRP systems. Management Science 32(4), 413-429. 5. **Kadipasaoglu & Sridharan (1995): freezing the MPS is most effective in multi-level systems under demand uncertainty.**
   - Earlier studies were mostly single-level and deterministic.
   - In a simulated multi-level system under demand uncertainty, the study compares freezing, end-item safety stock, and lot-for-lot below level 0.
   - Freezing the MPS is the most effective approach for both instability and cost "as the freeze length covers the cumulative lead time". It is also the least sensitive to operating conditions.

## Disagreements

- **Do forecast errors drive nervousness?** Ho & Ireland (1993) treat forecast errors as a source of nervousness via excessive rescheduling; their abstract is truncated before the result. Ho & Ireland (1998) find forecast errors "may not cause a higher degree of scheduling instability" and that lot-sizing choice matters more. This is the same lab, so the result is conditional on their simulation design.
- **Lot-for-lot as a dampener.**
  - Blackburn et al. (1986): lot-for-lot after stage 1 is not cost-effective against rolling-horizon nervousness.
  - Ho & Ho (1999): lot-for-lot at end-item level works only in certain environments.
  - Ho (1999/2002): PPB, Silver-Meal and LTC dampen nervousness.

  The studies differ in the source of nervousness (deterministic rolling horizon vs lead-time/demand uncertainty).
- **Horizon length.** Baker (1977), as restated by Carlson et al. (1982), found the longest horizon is not necessarily best. Carlson et al. (1982) find partial support for "more information the better". Stadtler (2000) makes performance horizon-insensitive through a model change.
- **Freezing cost.** Sridharan et al. (1987) find freezing up to 50% of the horizon costs little. Their own abstract notes that freezing "can also produce an increase in production and inventory costs". Atadeniz & Sridharan (2020) revisit this under capacity constraints, but the direction of their results was not retrieved.
- **Locus of instability.** Simulation studies focus on internal causes (lot sizing, horizon). Pujawan & Smart's (2012) practitioner survey says external buyer/supplier relationships dominate.
- **Single-lab caveat.** Most of the dampening and lot-sizing-as-dampener evidence (findings 8-15) comes from one author, C.-J. Ho. Treat it as a strong lead rather than independently replicated fact.

## Looked for, could not find

- **Steele (1975):** no abstract, full text or DOI. The content (e.g. tolerance-based dampening or message filtering) is unverified, and so is the volume/issue (16(4) is a guess).
- **Planner workload and message overload as a measured outcome:** no retrieved abstract quantifies reschedule-message counts as planner workload. The closest are Ho (2008), which says dampening filters "excessive rescheduling messages", and Minifie & Davis (1986). Specific dampening rules such as "ignore reschedules smaller than X periods" are not described in any retrieved abstract.
