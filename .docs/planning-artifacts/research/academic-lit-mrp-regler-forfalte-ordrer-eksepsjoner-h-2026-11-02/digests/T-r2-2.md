## Findings

1. **Gross requirements of a common part = sum over all parents (Q3).** Quote: "The gross requirements are the total demand derived from all parent production plans... The MPS start quantities for each parent are added to arrive at each week's gross requirements." The example is a seat subassembly (item C) used in both a ladder-back chair and a kitchen chair. | Krajewski, Malhotra & Ritzman, *Operations Management: Processes and Supply Chains*, 11th ed., ch. 11 "Efficient Resource Planning", ca. pp. 465-466 (Fig. 11.11) | Pearson | 2016 (©2015/2016) | accessed 2026-11-02 | https://archive.org/details/krajewski-lee-j.-malhotra-manoj-kumar-ritzman-larry-p-operations-management.-pro | high | definition/rule | primary (full-text OCR)

2. **The parent's planned order releases (not its gross requirements or planned receipts) drive the child's gross requirements (Q3).** Quote: "An item's gross requirements are derived from three sources: [1] The MPS for immediate parents that are end items 2. The planned order releases (not the gross requirements, scheduled receipts, or planned receipts) for immediate parents below the MPS level 3. Any other requirements not originating in the MPS, such as the demand for replacement parts." The same chapter also says components are withdrawn "from inventory at the beginning of the parent item's lead [time]", so they fall in the same period as the parent's release. | Krajewski et al. 11th ed., ch. 11, ca. pp. 466 and 471-472 | Pearson | 2016 | 2026-11-02 | same URL | high | rule | primary

3. **Action notices: release and reschedule of scheduled receipts (Q4).** Quote: "Inventory planners use a computer-generated memo called an action notice to make decisions about releasing new orders and adjusting the due dates of scheduled receipts." The notice flags "items that have a planned order release in the current period or a scheduled receipt that needs its due date revised." When a planned order is released, the planner's transaction "eliminates the planned order release in period 1, removes the planned receipt in period 2, and inserts a scheduled receipt for 230 units in period 2." The inventory-record transactions listed are "releasing new orders, receiving scheduled receipts, adjusting due dates for scheduled receipts, withdrawing inventory, canceling orders...". The text never uses the terms "reschedule in", "reschedule out" or "cancel" as separate message types. Its categories are release and due-date adjustment; canceling appears only as a transaction. | Krajewski et al. 11th ed., ch. 11, "Action Notices", ca. p. 473 (and "Inventory Record", ca. p. 465) | Pearson | 2016 | 2026-11-02 | same URL | high | exception-message taxonomy | primary

4. **MRP ignores capacity, so planners must adjust (Q1/Q4 context).** Quote: "By itself, the MRP system does not recognize capacity limitations when computing planned orders; that is, it may call for a planned order release that exceeds the amount that can be physically produced." | Krajewski et al. 11th ed., ch. 11, "Resource Requirements Reports", ca. p. 473 | Pearson | 2016 | 2026-11-02 | same URL | high | limitation | primary

5. **Netting with on-hand stock and scheduled receipts; release offset by lead time; lot-for-lot "may be... infeasible" (Q1 context, Q3).** In the Harmon Music trumpet example (Example 7.1), net requirements are found by "subtracting on-hand inventory and scheduled receipts from gross requirements." They "are then translated back in time by the production lead time" and lot-sized to give planned order releases. The valves' gross requirements are "the production schedule for the valve casing assembly by 3", i.e. the parent's planned order releases times the usage quantity, in the same week. Nahmias calls lot-for-lot "suboptimal and even infeasible", but only in the capacity sense (336 valves needed against a 200/week limit). The example is built so that every release falls inside the displayed weeks (demand starts in week 8, and the record columns start at weeks 2-6). Past-due releases are not discussed. | Nahmias, *Production and Operations Analysis*, 6th ed., ch. 7 "Push and Pull Production Control Systems: MRP and JIT", §7.2 "The Explosion Calculus", pp. 372-374 | McGraw-Hill/Irwin | 2009 | 2026-11-02 | https://archive.org/details/production-and-operations-analysis (djvu text) | high | worked method | primary (full-text OCR)

6. **Infeasible capacity loads → split lots into prior weeks or re-lot-size the parent (Q1/Q4).** Quote: "The capacity requirements show whether the planned order release obtained from the MRP is feasible... the current lot sizing is infeasible and some corrective action is required. One possibility is to split the lot scheduled for week 6 by producing some part of it in a prior week. Another is to adjust the lot sizing for the valve casing assembly at the next higher level." | Nahmias 6th ed., §7.5 "Shortcomings of MRP", Example 7.10, ca. pp. 390-391 | McGraw-Hill/Irwin | 2009 | 2026-11-02 | same URL | high | limitation/remedy | primary

7. **Planning horizon criterion (Q2): Nahmias does not use the cumulative-lead-time rule.** Quote: "When using rolling horizons, the planning horizon should be long enough to guarantee that the first-period decision does not change. 8. **Hopp & Spearman as reproduced in MIT 2.854 (Q3/Q4).** The slides name "Primary source: Factory Physics by Hopp and Spearman." Low-level code: "Items are given a low-level code corresponding to the lowest level they appear at, for any end item in the factory." Netting: inventory is projected forward period by period, and net requirements start at the first period where projected on-hand goes negative. The netting example has a row "Adjusted scheduled receipts" next to the original "Scheduled receipts" (10 in week 1, 100 in week 4). Outputs listed are "Planned order releases; Change notices; Exception reports -- discrepancies, consequences of unexpected events". ## Disagreements

- **Horizon rule (Q2):** Nahmias (6th ed., p. 391) sets the horizon by lot-sizing stability ("first-period decision does not change"), not by cumulative lead time. The cumulative-lead-time rule (as in Vollmann/Orlicky) was not corroborated in any text read this run. This is a difference in criterion, not a contradiction.
- **"Infeasible" (Q1):** Nahmias and Krajewski use "infeasible" only for capacity overloads. Neither uses it for a release whose date falls before the current period. Gershwin/Hopp-Spearman also mean capacity and determinism ("infinite capacity"). No textbook read this run labels past-due releases "infeasible".
- **Message taxonomy (Q4):** Krajewski has two categories (release new orders; adjust due dates of scheduled receipts), plus cancel as a transaction. Gershwin/Hopp-Spearman shows defer and expedite on scheduled receipts, and lists "change notices" and "exception reports" as outputs. The four-way list (release / reschedule in / reschedule out / cancel) was not found verbatim in these sources.
